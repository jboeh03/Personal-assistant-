## 1. Timeline mechanics

### 1.1 The governing clause text

**EVIDENCE — strict construction (§2):**
> "All timelines and deadlines under this Contract shall be strictly construed. Time is of the essence with regard to any and all timelines. Contract performance dates and contingencies in the Contract shall commence the day following Contract Acceptance Date."

**EVIDENCE — Consideration Period (§15(b); blank filled "3"):**
> "In the event that Buyer has timely delivered to Seller a Defect Notice, Seller shall have a period of up to **3** calendar days (the 'Consideration Period') beginning on the day following the delivery of the Defect Notice, to evaluate Buyer's request for correction(s)."

**EVIDENCE — deemed acceptance (§15(b), capitalized in the form):**
> "DELIVERY OF EITHER NOTICE IN THIS SECTION 15(b) SHALL DESIGNATE THE END OF THE CONSIDERATION PERIOD. IN THE EVENT THAT SELLER SHALL FAIL TO TIMELY PROVIDE ANY REQUIRED, WRITTEN NOTICE TO BUYER, SELLER SHALL BE DEEMED TO HAVE AGREED TO CORRECT DEFECTS IN THE MANNER DETAILED AND REQUESTED IN BUYER'S DEFECT NOTICE."

**EVIDENCE — Settlement Period and auto-termination (§15(c); blank filled "2"):**
> "In the event that Seller has timely delivered to Buyer a written counter-offer under Section 15(b)ii., the parties shall have up to **2** calendar days (the 'Settlement Period') beginning on the day following the delivery of such counter-offer, to reach a mutual, signed, written agreement detailing Seller's correction of defects, if any. … IN THE EVENT THAT THE PARTIES FAIL TO REACH A MUTUAL, SIGNED, WRITTEN AGREEMENT UNDER THIS SECTION 15(c), THIS CONTRACT SHALL AUTOMATICALLY TERMINATE."

**EVIDENCE — the Defect Notice form says the same (p.2):**
> "Delivery of Seller's written response to Buyer's Defect Notice constitutes the end of the Consideration Period. If Seller has not selected item (a) above, and has instead made a counteroffer to Buyer under item (b), (c), (d), (e) or (f) above during the Consideration Period, the Settlement Period shall commence. … If no agreement is reached during the Settlement Period, the Contract to Purchase shall automatically terminate."

**EVIDENCE — earnest money on termination (§3(a)):**
> "If this Contract terminates for any reason, the Earnest Money will NOT be automatically disbursed to any party."
> Broker-held branch (i): released only by a mutual signed Release or final court order; Ohio's 2-year rule then returns it to Buyer.
> Title-company branch (ii): "In the event that the parties … designate another third-party or the herein listed title company to hold the Earnest Money, the terms and conditions of this escrow hold shall be governed by separate, third-party escrow terms."

**INFERENCE (earnest money):** The $10,000 sits with **Fidelity Land Title** (branch (ii)): Fidelity's escrow terms govern release, but practically, on auto-termination nobody gets the $10K automatically — it takes a mutual signed Release or (in a dispute) a court order. The statutory 2-year return-to-buyer rule ([R.C. 4735.24](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-4735.24)) is quoted for the broker-held case; whether Fidelity's terms mirror it is unverified — **get a copy of Fidelity's escrow terms**. Either way: if this deal dies in a dispute, the $10K freezes; it doesn't pay the sellers.

## 2. Option (e) deep-dive — the escrow-holdback path

**EVIDENCE — the form text (Defect Notice p.2):**
> "(e) Seller offers to pay via title company check(s) the amounts specified, payable to the contractor(s) listed below and delivered to Buyer at Closing for work to be performed after Closing. Buyer shall assume responsibility for the corrections. If requested by the Buyer's lender or Seller, these funds shall be placed in escrow and be subject to a separate escrow agreement."

Two built-in features: (1) buyer assumes responsibility for the work — kills the "repairs complete prior to closing + re-inspection" execution risk; (2) the form itself anticipates the lender forcing a formal escrow.

### 2.1 Do conventional lenders permit this?

- **EVIDENCE:** Fannie Mae Selling Guide [B4-1.2-05, Requirements for Verifying Completion and Postponed Improvements](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-1.2-05/requirements-verifying-completion-and-postponed-improvements) contemplates loans closing before repairs are complete on existing construction with a completion escrow: minor/deferred-maintenance items eligible; **items adversely affecting safety, soundness, or structural integrity are ineligible**; escrow funded at **120% of estimated cost** (100% with fixed-price contract); completion generally within **180 days**; verified via **Form 1004D** ([Homebuyer.com summary](https://homebuyer.com/guidelines/fannie-mae/requirements-for-verifying-completion-and-postponed-improvements-b4-1-2-05); [AmeriSave](https://www.amerisave.com/glossary/escrow-holdback-what-it-means-for-home-buyers-in)). *Direct Selling Guide fetch was blocked; figures come from the secondary summaries and should be confirmed with the buyer's lender.*
- **EVIDENCE:** Freddie Mac parallel: Guide 5605 completion requirements and **Form 442** ([Homebuyer.com Freddie summary](https://homebuyer.com/guidelines/freddie-mac/requirements-for-properties-subject-to-completion-or-inspection-5605-8); [Freddie Mac Form 442](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/form/442)).
- **EVIDENCE:** Holdbacks are a recognized conventional device **at lender discretion**: "You must get the lender's approval in writing for any escrow holdback" ([Rocket Mortgage](https://www.rocketmortgage.com/learn/escrow-holdback)); conventional programs commonly cap escrowed repairs ~10% of as-completed value ([Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/blog/escrow-holdback/); [Premier Mortgage](https://www.premiermtg.com/west-palm-beach-escrow-holdbacks-closing-conventional-loans-after-minor-repairs/)). Some lenders simply refuse holdbacks (e.g., [LHFS policy](https://cdn.lhfs.com/lhfscdn/wholesale/download/EscrowHoldbackPolicy_Broker.pdf), [ARC Home policy](https://wholesale.archomellc.com/uploads/sites/17046/public/Escrow_Holdback_Policy_4_1_24_b.pdf)).

**Bottom line (INFERENCE):** Conventional underwriting *can* accommodate option (e), but only as a lender-approved, documented escrow — not informal "checks handed over at closing." Whether *this* buyer's lender allows it is the make-or-break unknown.

### 2.2 Typical sizing

- **EVIDENCE:** Fannie completion escrows: **120%** of estimated cost (above). VA requires **150% ("1.5×")** ([VA Circular 26-14-8](https://www.benefits.va.gov/HOMELOANS/documents/circulars/26_14_8.pdf)); many lenders/title companies apply 1.5× to conventional holdbacks as an overlay ([Rocket Mortgage](https://www.rocketmortgage.com/learn/escrow-holdback); [Barnes Walker](https://barneswalker.com/understanding-escrow-holdback/)).
- **INFERENCE:** Plan on **1.2×–1.5× the licensed-contractor bids** held from seller proceeds, surplus returned after completion sign-off. If roof + radon bids total $12–18K, that's roughly $15–27K temporarily tied up — check against the sellers' payoff/liquidity needs.

### 2.3 Documentation the lender/title company will want

1. Written itemized bids from licensed contractors (some lenders want two per item).
2. A written escrow/holdback agreement signed by buyer, seller, and escrow agent (Fidelity) — amount, funding source, completion deadline, disbursement conditions, who gets the overage.
3. Lender's written approval **before the Closing Disclosure is finalized** — the holdback must appear on the CD; undisclosed side agreements are mortgage-fraud territory.
4. Post-completion verification (Form 1004D/442 or equivalent).

### 2.4 The late-rejection risk

**INFERENCE:** The dangerous failure mode: agree to (e) on 08/10–08/12, then the buyer's underwriter rejects the holdback at conditional approval (~08/21) or clear-to-close (~08/25) — too late to revert to pre-closing repairs. Aggravators here: (a) if the **appraiser** notes the roof conditions and makes the appraisal "subject to repairs," pre-closing completion is forced regardless; (b) Fannie's "safety, soundness, or structural integrity" carve-out could arguably capture RS-2 sheathing. Radon mitigation, by contrast, is a classic escrowable item. **RECOMMENDATION:** if (e) is chosen, condition the counter on **written lender approval obtained during the Settlement Period** — have the buyers' agent put the question to the loan officer on 08/10–08/11, before signing — and consider a hybrid: escrow the roof work, but just *do* the radon mitigation pre-closing (fast, ~$1–2K, improves the disclosure posture permanently either way).

## 3. Option (c) price reduction vs. option (d) closing-cost credit

- **EVIDENCE — IPC caps:** Fannie Mae [B3-4.1-02, Interested Party Contributions](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-02/interested-party-contributions-ipcs): principal-residence caps **3% if LTV > 90%, 6% at 75.01–90%, 9% at ≤75%**; IPCs may only cover items normally the buyer's responsibility (closing costs/prepaids), cannot fund down payment; excess = sales concession → price adjusted down, LTV recalculated ([Enact summary](https://blog.enactmi.com/post/demystifying-interested-party-contributions-financing-concessions-and-sales-concessions); [Radian job aid](https://www.radian.com/-/media/Files/Enterprise/Training/QRG/uls-jobaid-ipcs-v3.pdf)).
- **INFERENCE — applied:** Buyers are at ≤90% LTV (§4) → the 6% band → cap ≈ **$35,100**. Any realistic defect credit ($5–20K) fits easily, *but* a (d) credit is doubly capped by the buyers' **actual** settlement charges + prepaids — dollars beyond actual costs are wasted. The base contract has $0 seller-paid settlement charges, so the whole 6% is headroom.
- **INFERENCE — which is cleaner:** For a 90%-LTV buyer, a **(d) closing-cost credit is cash-efficient** (reduces cash-to-close dollar-for-dollar up to actual costs), while a **(c) price cut** returns only ~10% as cash (the rest just shrinks their loan) — but (c) is **underwriting-proof**: no lender sign-off, slightly lower appraisal bar, cannot be rejected. Option (d)'s form framing as "settlement charges and/or other fees" is the right label (avoids "repair credit" red flags). **RECOMMENDATION:** if money-in-lieu is chosen, (d) sized within the buyers' actual closing costs is the buyer-friendliest fast yes; (c) is the fallback if their lender balks. Either resolves §15 with **zero execution risk** before 08/28 — no contractors, no re-inspection, no 1004D.

## 4. Disclosure duty — why the relist BATNA is weak (ORC 5302.30)

- **EVIDENCE:** [R.C. 5302.30](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5302.30) requires disclosure of material physical-condition matters "within the actual knowledge of the transferor," completed in good faith — including **radon gas** specifically (Ohio DOC form Section H). Safe harbor covers only what is NOT within the seller's actual knowledge ([Justia text](https://law.justia.com/codes/ohio/title-53/chapter-5302/section-5302-30/)).
- **EVIDENCE — amendment mechanics:** the form "may be amended in writing … at any time," and a buyer receiving the form or an amendment after signing gets a **3-business-day rescission right** with deposit return.
- **INFERENCE:** As of 08/04–08/07 the sellers have actual knowledge of: roof defects (RS-1–RS-6), **radon 13.6 pCi/L**, foundation cracks with visible movement + water signs (SS-1/SS-2), the Wadsworth panel, aged HVAC, rodent signs. The buyers' waiver applies to **this contract only** — it does not erase seller knowledge. On any relist the disclosure must be updated; answering "no known issues" would be a knowing misstatement outside the safe harbor.
- **EVIDENCE — consequences:** Ohio caveat emptor does not shield fraudulent concealment/misrepresentation of known latent defects; buyers can sue for fraud, damages, or rescission; "as is" doesn't defeat fraud claims ([KJK](https://kjk.com/2018/08/10/caveat-emptor-let-the-buyer-beware-is-still-alive-and-well-in-ohio-property-law/); [Nolo — Ohio](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/can-you-sue-home-seller-undisclosed-defects-ohio.html); [Cavell Law](https://www.cavelllaw.com/failure-to-disclose-real-estate/)). Contract §32 also indemnifies the REALTORS against seller concealment.
- **CONCLUSION (INFERENCE):** "Just relist" is not a reset to 07/31. Amended disclosure (radon 13.6, roof, foundation movement, panel, HVAC, rodents) → thinner, more skittish buyer pool, anchoring below the over-ask $585K, rescission rights, carrying costs through the separation, frozen earnest money. **Nearly any deal struck 08/10–08/12 beats termination — that asymmetry is the sellers' core negotiating fact.**

## 5. Risk flags (plain language)

1. **Blow the 08/10 deadline → you've "agreed" to everything.** Silence = deemed agreement to ALL defects exactly as written, pre-closing, by qualified professionals. The single worst unforced error available.
2. **Counter that stalls 2 days → dead contract.** Any (b)–(f) response starts a 2-calendar-day fuse; no mutual signed agreement by end of day 2 = automatic termination. Verbal deals don't count (§28: modifications invalid "UNLESS IN WRITING AND SIGNED BY ALL PARTIES").
3. **Responding *early* shortens your own fuse.** The Settlement Period runs from *your counter's* delivery. Time the response deliberately (08/09–08/10 morning).
4. **Repair-completion risk under (a)/(b).** Option (a) requires corrections completed **prior to closing**, workmanlike, by qualified professionals, receipts due at the buyers' re-inspection **~08/25**. That's ~2 weeks to bid, schedule, and finish roof work + radon in August; an unfinished item on 08/25 hands the buyers leverage at the worst moment.
5. **Scope ambiguity in RS-2.** The notice says "repair affected sheathing"; the inspection itself says such issues are "commonly reconciled when failure occurs or when new roof coverings are installed." Agreeing to (a) unamended risks a re-inspection dispute over whether spot-repair satisfies "in the manner detailed and requested."
6. **Option (e)'s hidden veto: the buyer's lender.** Written approval, documented bids, escrow agreement, 1.2–1.5× funding, CD disclosure; rejection can land ~08/21–08/25, too late to pivot. Condition any (e) counter on lender sign-off inside the Settlement Period.
7. **Appraisal can override everything.** If the appraiser flags the roof, the appraisal comes back "subject to repairs" and pre-closing completion is forced regardless of the §15 deal. (The §19 appraisal-gap addendum covers *value* up to $10K, not *condition*.)
8. **Earnest money is leverage that freezes, not pays.** On termination the $10K at Fidelity moves only by mutual Release or court order; the contract's 2-year default rule favors the buyer in a stalemate. Get Fidelity's escrow terms.
9. **Termination ≠ clean relist** (see §4).
10. **What helps the sellers:**
    - **§23 7-day closing extension (EVIDENCE):** "…in the event that Buyer and Seller are proceeding in good faith performance under this Contract and Closing cannot occur due to occurrence or circumstance out of the direct control of either party, the date of Closing shall be extended for a period of up to 7 calendar days." *Limits (INFERENCE):* requires good-faith performance and an outside cause — a contractor no-show arguably qualifies; seller foot-dragging does not. A shock absorber, not a plan.
    - **Buyer posture:** over-ask, $10K earnest, $10K gap coverage, focused ask that waived foundation/panel/HVAC — buyers who want the house; a fast papered agreement is very achievable.
    - **The option-(e) form text** pre-authorizes the escrow structure "if requested by the Buyer's lender or Seller" — the form anticipates the cleanest compromise.
