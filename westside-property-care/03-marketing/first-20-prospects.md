## 1. What this file is, and what it deliberately is not

The owner's own work-block plan calls for **"first 20 prospects / target properties"**, filtered
by **pools, outdoor kitchens, large patios, landscaping, and high-value homes**. This file is
that filter, applied.

**It is not a populated list of real addresses.** No agent working on this project has
street-level data on which specific west-side houses have a pool or a built outdoor kitchen, and
inventing twenty addresses to make this file look finished would be worse than leaving it a
worksheet — it would put fabricated data in front of a homeowner's door.
`neighborhood-campaign.md` §2.3 already makes this call for the wider street-selection process;
this file applies the identical reasoning to the specific first-20 exercise. **What follows is
the method and the structure to fill in, using the real research channels available, not a
finished roster.**

---

## 2. The filter, from his own plan

Four things, applied together, not separately:

1. **A pool.** Visible from the street, satellite imagery, or the county auditor's recorded
   improvements.
2. **An outdoor kitchen.** A built-in grill, a stone or masonry counter, a covered cooking
   structure — visible the same three ways.
3. **A large patio, or extensive landscaping.** Multiple seating zones, a substantial hardscape
   footprint, mature and maintained beds — these are signals of an owner who already invests in
   and maintains outdoor space, which is qualifying attribute 2 in `ideal-client.md` §2.2
   ("already pays someone to maintain something").
4. **A high-value home**, inside the qualified price band for its area (`ideal-client.md` §3):
   Green Township $250–450k, upper Delhi within the $180–350k range specifically at the top end,
   Covedale above the west-side median, Oak Hills' established higher-value stock.

**A property does not need all four to make the list — it needs enough of them to be worth
driving past.** A pool alone, in the right price band, is enough to add a candidate row. A large
patio with no pool or kitchen is a weaker signal and belongs lower on the list, consistent with
`ideal-client.md` §2.5 (properties with neither attribute are the weakest client shape in the
book, capped at one of six).

## 3. Crossed with the qualified geography

Only these areas, per `ideal-client.md` §3 — never Westwood, Price Hill, or Cheviot, regardless
of what's visible on a given street:

| Area | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Green Township subdivisions** | The core target — $250–450k, the highest density of in-ground pools and built outdoor kitchens on the west side. Green Township includes Bridgetown, Covedale, Dent, Mack, Monfort Heights, and White Oak as named communities. Newer developments in the northwestern part of the township are worth checking first for concentration of pools and built kitchens; established sections of Monfort Heights, Dent, and White Oak are worth checking for older in-ground pools and additions. |
| **Oak Hills** | Established, well-kept streets with mature outdoor investment — older pools and additions, not new construction. |
| **Upper Delhi** | The top of the $180–350k range only — river-side and higher-value streets specifically, not Delhi generally. Delhi Hills, along the Ohio River, is publicly known as one of the higher-value pockets of Delhi Township and is worth checking first. |
| **Covedale** | Well-maintained, owner-occupied pockets above the west-side median, including newer developments aimed at move-up buyers and empty-nesters. |

**Bridgetown is conditional.** It sits inside Green Township at ~$240k, the bottom of the
qualified range — a Bridgetown candidate only makes this list if a specific property clears the
attribute filter in §2, not because the street is inside Green Township generally.

**A note on how these area names were sourced.** The subdivision and community names above come
from general public knowledge of west-side Cincinnati geography — township boundaries, named
communities, and broadly known pockets of higher-value housing stock. They are starting points
for the research method in §4, not a confirmation that any specific street or subdivision
actually has qualifying properties on it. That confirmation only comes from the three research
steps below.

## 4. The research method — do this before driving anything

Same order as `neighborhood-campaign.md` §2.3, applied specifically to filling in the worksheet
in §6:

1. **Hamilton County Auditor property records** (publicly searchable) — check recorded
   improvements on candidate parcels inside the areas in §3. In-ground pools are frequently
   recorded as an assessed feature, and this is the fastest way to build a candidate list before
   ever leaving the desk.
2. **Satellite and street-level map imagery** — confirms or rules out what the auditor records
   suggest, and surfaces outdoor kitchens, covered patios, and large hardscape footprints that
   don't show up in assessment records at all.
3. **The actual drive.** Nothing replaces seeing it — a pool cover, a stone counter, a
   well-maintained bed line are only fully confirmed from the curb.
4. **The TSGC warm list, if confirmed** (`warm-list-campaign.md`). Every qualified-geography
   address on that list is a pre-identified candidate before any of the above research even
   starts — fold it in first if it's available.

## 5. Route compactness and the composition caps, applied at the list stage

Two rules from `operating-model.md` §4 and `ideal-client.md` §2.3 apply as soon as this list has
more than a couple of candidates — not just at the point of signing:

- **Cluster the candidates, don't scatter them.** As candidates accumulate, group them
  geographically rather than treating the list as twenty independent leads. A tight group of
  five candidates on adjacent streets is worth more than five scattered across four
  subdivisions, because clients 1 and 2 get chosen for cluster potential
  (`ideal-client.md` §4) — the list should make that choice easy, not require redoing the
  legwork later.
- **Flag every candidate that looks like it has both a pool and an outdoor kitchen.** The book
  caps at 2 of 6 such properties, and never two on the same route day
  (`ideal-client.md` §2.3, `unit-economics.md` §2.3). If a cluster of five candidates turns out
  to have three "both" properties, that cluster cannot become the whole route — at most two of
  those three can ever be signed, and they can't share a route day. Flag this at the worksheet
  stage so it's visible before any conversation happens, not discovered after three of them have
  already said yes.

**Column notes:**

- **Street / area (general)** — a street name or a small block, not a house number, until a
  candidate becomes a real outreach target. This keeps the worksheet from reading as a target
  list of specific households before any qualification has actually happened.
- **Cluster** — assign `A` or `B` once two or more candidates are close enough to plausibly share

  strong signals; `low` for a single weak signal (large patio only, no pool or kitchen
  confirmed) — these are the §2.5 weak-signal candidates and belong at the bottom of the working
  order, not off the list entirely.

## 7. What to do once this worksheet has real rows

1. Sort by cluster, then priority — work the tightest, highest-signal cluster first, since it's
   also the one most likely to produce clients 1 and 2 (`ideal-client.md` §4).
2. Move confirmed candidates into `intake-sheet.md` as `New` rows, with `Pool Y/N` and
   `Outdoor Kitchen Y/N` filled in from the confirmed signal, not the general "signal seen"
   column here.
3. Start the door-hanger pass (`neighborhood-campaign.md` §5) or the qualifying conversation on
   the highest-priority cluster, per `gtm-plan.md` §3, Phase 1.
4. Once client 1 signs, re-run this worksheet centered on their street, per
   `neighborhood-campaign.md` §2.4 — the next twenty candidates come from proximity to a real
   client, not from a second cold desk-research pass.
