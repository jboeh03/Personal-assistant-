## 1. The one thing — *the sixteenth visit*

> **Sixteen visits. Six properties. The same person every time, who writes down what he saw.**

That is the headline form, locked. `CANON.md` §4 proposes it; this file adopts it verbatim and
does not improve it.

**And this is the sentence underneath it that does the actual argumentative work:**

> **You are not buying sixteen visits. You are buying the sixteenth one** — the visit where he
> already knows which gate sticks, which well fills, and what the maple does in September,
> because he wrote down the other fifteen.

Call it **the sixteenth visit**. Downstream agents should cite it by that name.

---

### 1.1 What this argument replaced, and why it had to

The old argument was *"the same price in January as in July."* It was doing one specific job:
explaining why this is a **membership** — a standing relationship you pay for on a schedule —
rather than a **seasonal service** you buy when you need it. Its logic was that continuity of
price across the dead months proves you are buying a relationship, not a stack of visits.

The owner's calendar ended it. There is no January billing. There is no January visit. Saying
the old line now would be a false statement about the price, and every one of its ~30
appearances is a defect (`voice.md` §8, item 20 — retired for exactly this reason).

**The failure mode if nothing replaces it** is worse than an empty slot in the copy. Without a
membership argument, $279 × 8 months reads as **$2,232 for sixteen appointments**, and sixteen
appointments is a punch card. A punch card gets compared on price per appointment, which is
$139.50, which invites the arithmetic `voice.md` §3 bans and loses to a lawn service, a pool
service, and a handyman simultaneously. The membership frame is not decoration. It is what keeps
the offer from being priced as a commodity.

### 1.2 Why *the sixteenth visit* does the same job

The old argument located the value in **what he charges when he isn't there.** The new one
locates it in **what accumulates while he is.** Both make the recurring relationship the product
rather than the delivery mechanism, which is the job. The new one is better on three counts.

1. **It is true and it is checkable.** Every element is already a fact: sixteen visits
   (Agreement §1, §8), six properties (`CANON.md` §3), one person, and a written summary every
   visit (Checklist §8). Nothing had to be invented and nothing depends on a policy that could
   change.
2. **It cannot be copied.** A pool company with two hundred accounts can match the price, can
   match the frequency, and can match the season. It cannot send the same person sixteen times
   to the same six properties and have him remember the west window well. The argument is
   downstream of the cap, which is the one structural thing about this business a competitor
   cannot replicate without becoming a different business.
3. **It survives the season instead of fighting it.** The old argument had to defend the winter.
   This one is *about* a season — sixteen is a number that only exists because the season has a
   shape. The seasonality stops being the objection and becomes the unit of measure.

### 1.3 The four legs, from `CANON.md` §4

Each is a fact, each has a source, and each has a stated failure condition.

| Leg | The claim | Source | What makes it a lie |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Six properties, ever** | The cap is real and it is the product. | `CANON.md` §3 | A seventh client. The moment that happens the whole argument goes. |
| **The same person every visit** | No rotating crew, no route number, no dispatcher, no subcontractor. | `CANON.md` §1 | Sending anyone else, once. |
| **A written record** | Sixteen documented inspections a season, with photos where they matter. | Checklist §1, §8 | A summary that reads "routine care completed." The claim lives or dies in the summary, not on the website. |
| **Scope stated up front** | *Maintain everything. Deep-clean nothing unless specifically scheduled.* He tells you what he does not do before you sign. | Checklist footer; Agreement §§2–7 | An exclusion that appears in the agreement but not on the website. |

### 1.4 The honest limit that ships with it

Say it in the same breath, every time. It is `voice.md` rule 5 and it is what makes the rest
believable.

> Sixteen visits, March 1 through October 31. November through February there are no scheduled
> visits and I'm not billing you for any.

Do not soften it, do not bury it below the price, and do not let a surface imply otherwise. A
reader who discovers the season after they have read the price will discount everything above it.

## 2. The three supports

Each support answers a different question, carries its own proof, and has a stated failure
condition. Downstream agents: if you cannot point at the proof, do not make the claim.

---

### Support 1 — He knows *your* property, and it compounds

**The claim:** By visit six he can tell you which gate sticks, which window wells fill, and which
pot on the north side dries out first. By visit sixteen he can tell you what is going to be a
problem next March. Six properties is few enough to hold in his head, and sixteen written records
mean he does not have to.

**Why it matters:** specificity is the entire brand (`CANON.md` §8) and it is the reason this
beats a big company. A route tech with forty stops cannot name a fixture on your property. He can.
This is the direct load-bearing support for *the sixteenth visit*.

**The proof:**

| Evidence | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Six clients maximum | `CANON.md` §3 |
| Sixteen visits a season, two a month, March 1 – October 31 | Agreement §1, §8 |
| The same person every visit — no crew, no dispatch, no subcontractor | `CANON.md` §1 |
| Arrival walkthrough and final walk on every visit, both written down | Checklist blocks 1 and 8 |
| A short written summary sent to the customer at the end of every visit | Checklist block 8 |
| A standing `NOTES / ITEMS TO QUOTE / FOLLOW-UP` block carried on the checklist itself | Checklist, page 2 |

**The line:**
> Sixteen visits a season on six properties. By the fourth one I know which gate sticks on yours.

**What makes this false:** a summary that says "routine care completed."

---

### Support 2 — The scope is written down, including what is *not* in it

**The claim:** Everything he does not do is published before you are asked to sign anything — and
he walks it out loud before he gives you a price. His own field checklist is footed with the scope
limit, not just the contract.

**Why it matters:** this is the highest-trust move available to a business with no track record,
it costs nothing, and almost nobody in the category does it. It also filters out the client who
would be a problem in month three.

**The proof:**

| Evidence | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| *Maintain everything. Deep-clean nothing unless specifically scheduled* — printed on the document he carries | Checklist footer, both pages |
| *No guarantee of water chemistry, water clarity, equipment performance, or pool-system operation* | Agreement §3 |
| *Completion of every possible task during every visit is not guaranteed* | Agreement §5 |
| *Work beyond the scheduled service time or outside routine service requires Client approval before additional billable work is performed* | Agreement §6 |
| Deep cleaning, repairs, parts, restoration, and disassembly named as excluded and quotable | Agreement §4, §6 |
| Materials, chemicals, salt, filters, and consumables named as additional | Agreement §7 |
| One published price, no configuration table | `CANON.md` §3 |

**The line:**
> Everything I don't do is on this page, above the price. Water chemistry, lawn, gutters, deep
> cleans — I'll tell you who to call.

**What makes this false:** an exclusion that appears only in the agreement and not on the website,
or a price page that says "contact us."

---

### Support 3 — Six, and it is a real cap

**The claim:** The book stops at six properties. When they are full the site says MEMBERSHIP FULL
and offers a waitlist that is an actual list.

**Why it matters:** the open capacity is not slack he failed to sell, it is part of the product. It
buys the weather float, the extra time on a property that needs it, the estimate inside the week,
and the ability to keep the standard that matters most: never rush a property because someone else
is waiting.

**The proof:**

| Evidence | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Launch at 4, hard cap at 6 | `CANON.md` §3 |
| Routine visits only on Monday and Friday, on the schedule set with each client | `CANON.md` §3 |
| Tuesday–Thursday protected for projects, estimates, and emergencies | `CANON.md` §3 |
| No regularly scheduled weekend work | `CANON.md` §3 |
| The site shows the true count of open slots, and MEMBERSHIP FULL when it is zero | `CANON.md` §3 |
| The two-hour cap plus the heavier pool scope is what sizes the route | `CANON.md` §3 |

**The line:**
> Six is the cap because six is what fits on Mondays and Fridays without me hurrying at your house
> to get to somebody else's.

**What makes this false:** a seventh client. Never quietly take one because someone was persistent.
The moment that happens, this support and the whole positioning go with it.

### The section order that must not change

On any surface carrying a price, the order is:

1. What happens on a visit
2. **What is not included** — including the pool disclaimer
3. The season: March 1 – October 31, sixteen visits
4. The price, with the two-hour cap and both its clauses
5. How billing and cancellation work
6. The ask

The exclusions and the season both go **above** the price. A page that quotes $279 and then
discloses that the season ends October 31 has already lost the trust the disclosure was supposed
to buy — and it is the disclosure most likely to be discovered by a reader who feels misled.

## 4. How to talk about price

**One number. Say it flat, in a sentence, and do not qualify it.**

> **$279 a month. That's the whole price list.**

**Directly under it, always:**

> Two scheduled visits a month, March 1 through October 31 — sixteen visits a season. Up to two
> hours of on-site service each visit, highest priority first; completion of every possible task
> every visit isn't guaranteed, and unused time doesn't roll over. Card on file, charged on the
> schedule we set when you sign. Thirty days' written notice to cancel, either of us, and there's
> no term.

**The flatness is worth one sentence of its own, because it is unusual:**

> A property with a pool and an outdoor kitchen pays the same as a property with a plain patio.

**And the consequence, said honestly if it comes up:** because the price does not move with the
property, the property decides *whether he takes the client*, not what he charges
(`CANON.md` §3). A pool-and-kitchen property consumes far more of the two hours. Say that plainly
on the walkthrough. Never write copy implying a pool costs extra, and never write copy implying a
pool property gets turned away for having a pool.

**Never:**

- Any of the dead prices: $229, $249 *(as a membership price)*, $269, $289, $329.
- "Starting at," "from," "plans," "tiers," "packages," "options," "add-ons," "modules."
- Any division of $279 by hours, visits, weeks, or days. (`voice.md` §3, the hours-arithmetic rule)
- Any discount, prepay rate, annual rate, referral credit, or last-slot price.

**Founding terms, when they apply:**

> The season's already underway, so I'll pro-rate what's left of it and lock your rate for all of
> next year. That isn't a promotion — it's what I'm giving the people who take a chance on a
> business with no track record.

> 🔶 **Flag, don't guess.** `CANON.md` §3 says "clients signing now." ⚠ The stale `pricing.md` §2
> says "the first 4." Whether the lock is capped at four members is unresolved. Until
> `wpc-strategist` resolves it, say "clients signing now" and never assert a cap.

**Published project prices** — these two, and only these two, appear in customer-facing material:

- Grill and outdoor kitchen deep clean — **$249+**
- Window well cleanout — **$49+**

Everything else is "quoted after I look at it." Never publish the internal anchors.

**Member project note, worth saying out loud:**

> Members don't get a discount on project work. They get scheduling priority and no trip minimum —
> a $49 window well is worth doing when I'm already standing in the yard, and it isn't worth a
> dedicated trip when I'm not.

## 5. Objection handling

### 5.1 "Why wouldn't I just hire a lawn guy?"

**Behind it:** They have not yet understood that this is a different category. They are pricing it
against what a lawn service charges per cut, which is the wrong comparison entirely.

**Say:**
> A lawn guy cuts your grass. I don't cut grass at all — no mowing, no trimming, no edging, no
> beds. What I take care of is the part of the yard you actually sit in: the pool area, the patio,
> the outdoor kitchen, the furniture, and the hardscape around them.
>
> And the lawn guy isn't looking at anything. I walk the property at the start of every visit and
> again at the end, and I write down what I found. Sixteen times a season. That's how you hear
> about the split hose bib in June instead of about the flooded basement in February.
>
> Keep your lawn guy. He and I aren't doing the same job.

**Never say:** anything that positions him as better than a lawn service, or any line implying he
does lawn or landscape work.

**Source:** Agreement §2 · ⚠ `service-catalog.md` §4.2 (clause language still valid) ·
`brand-brief.md` §6

---

### 5.2 "That's a lot per month."

**Behind it:** Two different people say this. One is doing arithmetic and cannot get there — that
person is a project client or a waitlist name, and the conversation stays friendly. The other wants
this service at $200 — that person is a disqualifier.

**Say:**
> It is. And it's one number — $279 whether you've got a pool and an outdoor kitchen or a plain
> patio.
>
> Here's what it buys that isn't obvious. It's sixteen visits a season, not two. It's the same
> person every one of them, so by the fourth I know your property, and by the sixteenth I know what
> it's going to need next March. And it's the reason I catch things — the split hose bib, the
> downspout dumping against the foundation, the tear in the pool cover you'd have found in April.
> Half the value is the visits and half is that somebody is looking and writing it down.
>
> If it isn't the right fit, I'd still be glad to do the grill for you. That's $249 and up, and it
> doesn't commit you to anything.

**Never say:** any discount, any trial rate, any "let me see what I can do." The membership price
is never discounted, including for the last slot.

**Source:** `CANON.md` §3 · §1 of this file

### 5.3 🆕 "$279 for four hours is $70 an hour. That's more than a handyman."

**Behind it:** This is a new objection and it is the sharpest one on the list, because **his own
agreement sells hours** — *"two scheduled property-care visits each month, with up to two hours of
on-site service per visit."* The arithmetic is correct and he cannot pretend it isn't. He also
cannot change the price. So the answer has to be a genuine reframe, not a dodge.

Three things make it work: **concede the number**, **explain what the cap is for**, and **name what
the hourly comparison leaves out.**

**Say:**
> $69.75, if you want to be exact. You're right about the arithmetic.
>
> Here's the part the arithmetic misses. A handyman's hour starts when you call him. You have to
> notice the problem, describe it, get on his schedule, and be home. Mine starts because it's the
> first Monday — I show up whether you noticed anything or not, and most of what I find, you
> hadn't. That's the thing you're actually buying.
>
> The two hours is a ceiling, not a quota. It's there so the price can be one number: a property
> with a pool and an outdoor kitchen would take three hours to do everything, and a plain patio
> would take one, and I'd rather cap the time than charge you differently for owning a pool. So I
> spend those two hours on whatever will make the biggest difference that day, highest priority
> first, and I tell you what I didn't get to. Completion of every possible task every visit isn't
> guaranteed — that's in the agreement in those words, because I'd rather you read it than find
> out.
>
> If what you want is hours, I'd honestly rather sell you a project. Those are flat-priced — a
> grill and outdoor kitchen deep clean from $249, pressure washing quoted after I look at it — and
> you know exactly what you're getting for the money. The membership is a different thing. It's
> sixteen visits and a record.

**Never say:**
- Any per-hour figure for the membership as a *justification*: "that's cheaper than X per hour,"
  "worth every penny at $70/hr." Conceding the reader's arithmetic once is honest; producing your
  own is the banned move. (`voice.md` §3, the hours-arithmetic rule)
- "But I usually stay longer than two hours." Never. It contradicts the cap, it sets an
  expectation, and the next visit that runs ninety minutes becomes a complaint.
- "It's more like $X once you count the report." Do not monetize the summary.
- Any offer of more hours, an extra visit, or a longer visit to close the sale.
- The internal $100/hour project quoting rate. It is internal.

**Source:** Agreement §1, §5 · `CANON.md` §3 · `voice.md` §3, the hours-arithmetic rule, and
pair 19

### 5.4 🔁 "What happens in winter?"

**Rewritten 2026-08-05, and the frame is inverted.** This used to be *the* objection to defeat —
the single biggest threat to the business, because a member cancelling in February took the whole
season with them. **It is no longer an objection.** It is a fact the customer accepts before they
sign, and the only way to get it wrong now is to be cagey about it.

**Behind it:** a straightforward scheduling question, usually asked in a normal tone. Treat it that
way. Any defensiveness in the answer invents a problem the customer did not have.

**Say:**
> Nothing scheduled. The season runs March 1 through October 31 — that's sixteen visits — and the
> last one is the end of October. November through February there are no scheduled visits, and
> I'm not billing you for any of them.
>
> What you get instead is the last visit of the season, which is the one where I put the covers on,
> tell you what I'd want handled before it gets cold, and price it.
>
> Two things carry on through the winter if you want them. Project work runs year round — a grill
> deep clean before the holidays, pressure washing while the deck is empty, storm cleanup. And
> there's a separate optional winter service I offer, which is its own thing with its own price;
> it's not part of this agreement and it isn't included.
>
> Then your next scheduled visit is the first route day in March.

**Never say:**
- Anything implying the membership covers November through February.
- Anything describing the winter offering as "included," "part of your membership," or
  "year-round protection." It is a separate, optional product (`CANON.md` §3).
- A winter retainer, an off-season rate, a service credit, or rolled-over unused time.
- Season-closing urgency: "last chance before the season ends," "only three visits left."
  (`voice.md` §6.5)
- Anything defending the season, apologizing for it, or explaining why it *has* to be this way.
  It is his calendar. State it.

> 🔶 **Do not describe the winter offering's scope or price.** `03-marketing/winter-service.md` is
> referenced by `CANON.md` §3 but **has not been written yet.** Until `wpc-marketing` writes it,
> the only permitted description is: *a separate optional winter service, its own price, not part
> of this agreement.* Do not invent visits, tasks, or a number.

**Source:** `CANON.md` §3 · Agreement §1, §8 · `voice.md` pair 11 and pair 18

### 5.5 "Why is there a waitlist? / Are you really full?"

**Behind it:** Reasonable suspicion. Manufactured scarcity is everywhere and they have been burned

**Say:**
> Six properties is twelve visits a month, and I only do routine visits on Mondays and Fridays.

> Two things actually move you up: a spot opening near you, or a neighbour of yours joining the
> list. If two of you are on the same street, that's a cluster and it changes the math.

> Meanwhile I do project work Tuesday through Thursday whether you're a member or not.

**Never say:** "spots are filling fast," "exclusive," "invitation only," or any count that is not
the true count.

### 5.7 "You're brand new. Why should I trust you?"

**Behind it:** The real objection under most of the others. It cannot be answered with credentials

> What I can tell you is that I'm local, I've got military roots, and what I'm not new at is

> The first month is the whole risk. If it's not right, tell me.

**Never say:** years of experience, "established," a client count, a testimonial, a review count, a

(`brand-brief.md` §4.2, §4.4).

### 5.8 🔁 "Can I pay less in a month you're only there twice?"

**Replaces the retired "Can I pause it over the winter?"** There is no winter to pause. What
survives is the same instinct — renegotiating the number — pointed at a different month.

**Say:**
> No, and I'd rather explain than just say no. It's twice a month every month of the season, so
> there isn't a light month to discount. The price is one number and it's the same for everybody,
> which is the only way I can charge a pool-and-outdoor-kitchen property the same as a plain patio.
>
> If a visit gets rained out I move it, usually to the next Monday or Friday, and it stays in the
> same month where I can. Nothing changes on the billing for that either.
>
> If what you want is warm-weather work without a membership, I'd genuinely rather sell you
> projects — a grill deep clean before you host, pressure washing in May, opening and closing the
> pool. That's a real option and I'm not saying it to be difficult.

**Never say:** anything that leaves the door open. No "let me think about it," no "maybe for you."

**Source:** `CANON.md` §3 · Agreement §8, §11

---

### 5.9 "Can you come more often? / Can you come on Wednesday?"

**Say:**
> Two visits a month is the membership, and they land on your route day — a Monday or a Friday,
> set when you sign. I don't add visits and I don't do a specific requested date, because the day I
> start doing that is the day I start rushing somebody.
>
> Tuesday through Thursday I'm doing projects and estimates, and that's deliberately kept open. So
> if something comes up between visits, there's a decent chance I can get to it midweek. I won't
> promise same-day and I'd rather tell you that now than have you find out in July.

**Never say:** a response window, a guaranteed turnaround, or "just call me any time."

**But do not strip the reply commitment when you strip the response window.** They are different
promises and `voice.md` §8 splits them at items 14a and 14b. Promising that *work* gets done inside
a stated period is banned — "within 24 hours," "same-day service." Promising that *he answers* is
required wherever someone is left waiting, and it is said in his own hours with the failure case
attached: *"I'll answer the same day or the next morning. If it's after Thursday, it might be
Monday."*

**Source:** `CANON.md` §3 · Agreement §8 · `voice.md` pair 17

---

### 5.10 "What time will you be there?"

**Say:**
> I'll tell you the day, not the hour. That's on purpose — if I give you an eleven o'clock, then
> the property before yours gets shortchanged when it needs an extra half hour, and eventually
> yours does too. Arrival times move for weather, traffic, and whatever the property before you
> turned out to need. Your visits are on {{route_day}}. You don't need to be home.

**Never say:** a window, "between," "morning," or "I'll text when I'm on my way" as a standing
promise.

**Source:** Agreement §8 · `CANON.md` §3

---

### 5.11 "Will you do the front too? / What about the garage?"

**Say:**
> The membership covers the outdoor living area — patio, pool and pool deck, outdoor kitchen, the
> hardscape around them, and the walkway that serves them. We'll walk it together on the first
> visit and write down exactly what's in and what isn't, so there's nothing to argue about later.
>
> Front of the house, lawn, wooded part of the lot, garage, sunroom, anything indoors — not in it.
> If you want something out there handled, I'll quote it.
>
> One thing that is on you: I need reasonable access on your route day — gates, the equipment pad,
> an outlet, a water source — and pets secured where I need to work.

**Never say:** "sure, I can grab that while I'm here."

**Source:** Agreement §2, §9 · ⚠ `service-catalog.md` §4.3 (clause language still valid)

### 5.12 🔁 "Why wouldn't I just hire a pool company?"

**Rewritten 2026-08-05, and this is the hardest objection in the business.** `CANON.md` §5 states
the problem exactly: the old scope excluded nearly all pool work, so the $121–142 market rate was
easy to wave off as a different product. **The new scope includes vacuuming, brushing, backwashing,
and salt-cell checks** — which puts $279 in direct comparison with a specialist at roughly half the
price, doing the same visible tasks twice as often, with chemicals included.

**The answer cannot be a deflection any more, and it must not be a comparison he claims to win.**
The structure is: lead with his own disclaimer, concede the pool company is better at the pool, then
say what he is actually selling.

**Say:**
> Because I'm not a pool company, and I'd rather you heard that from me.
>
> Here's my own disclaimer, out of my agreement: **I don't guarantee water chemistry, water
> clarity, equipment performance, or pool-system operation.** I don't test or balance water and I
> don't add chlorine, shock, salt, or algaecide. If it goes green in July, that is not something
> I'm responsible for or able to fix.
>
> A pool company will do that, weekly, with chemicals, for something like $121 to $142 a month
> around here. That is a real product, it's less than I charge, and if what you need is somebody
> accountable for the water, you should hire one. I'll tell you which ones your neighbours use.
>
> What I do on the pool is thirty minutes of a two-hour visit: skim, brush the steps and corners,
> vacuum, empty both baskets and the robot container, backwash if the system wants it, check the
> salt reading and the warning lights, and look the equipment over. That's real work and it's worth
> having. But it's one block out of eight — because the rest of the visit is the deck, the
> furniture, the outdoor kitchen, the grill, the grease trap, the trash cans, the pots, the sticks
> and the leaves, and the walk-through at both ends that produces the written summary.
>
> So it isn't half a pool service at twice the price. It's a property service that includes the
> pool area. Plenty of people have both of us. If you can only have one and the water is what keeps
> you up at night, have the pool company.

**Why the last line is in there and must stay:** offering to lose the sale is the only move that
makes the disclaimer above it credible. A prospect who is told "hire the other guy if that's what
you need" and stays is a prospect who understood the scope — which is exactly the client
`CANON.md` §5 says he needs.

**Never say:**
- "More thorough than a pool company," "everything a pool company does plus…," or any claim that
  positions him as the better pool option.
- Any subtraction or comparison arithmetic: "$279 minus what a pool company costs," "you're only
  really paying $150 for everything else." (`voice.md` §3, the hours-arithmetic rule and the
  anchor-leak rule)
- The $121–142 figure without attaching it to **full service with chemistry**. Attached loosely, it
  reads as "his competitor charges less for the same thing."
- "Complete pool care," "full pool service," "we keep your pool clear," "swim-ready," or anything
  in the pool rows of `voice.md` §6.4.
- Anything softening the disclaimer, and anything that puts it below the task list rather than
  above it.

**Source:** `CANON.md` §5 · Agreement §3 · Checklist block 2 · `voice.md` pair 5

### 5.15 "Can I get a discount for paying the season up front?"

**Say:**
> I appreciate it, but no. The price is the price and it's the same for everybody — that's the
> whole point of it being one number.
>
> What I do have is the founding terms. The season's already underway, so I'll pro-rate what's left
> of it and lock your rate for all of next year. If you're signing now, that's yours.

**Never say:** any prepay discount, any bundled discount, any "for you," any annual rate.

### 5.16 🆕 "Can I start in March instead?"

**Behind it:** Usually genuine, occasionally a soft no. Either way it costs him the rest of the
current season, and the honest answer is the one that also makes the case.

**Say:**
> You can, and I'll hold a spot if one's open. But I'd rather start you now, and here's the honest
> reason: the first visit or two on a property is mostly me learning it. If we start in March, the
> March and April visits are the ones where I'm still finding out where the wells are and which
> gate sticks. If we start now, March is a visit where I already know.
>
> That's most of what you're paying for. Sixteen visits is the number, but it's the later ones that
> are worth the money.
>
> Either way I'm pro-rating what's left of this season, so you're only paying for the visits you
> actually get.

**Never say:** "spots won't be there in March," "you'll lose your rate," or anything else that
manufactures a deadline. `{{slots_open}}` is the only number that may be used here, and only if it
is the true one.

**Source:** §1 of this file · `CANON.md` §3 · `voice.md` §6.5

## 6. What to lead with, by lead source

Three channels, and only three.

| Lead source | Lead with | Because |
|---|---|---|
| **Neighbour referral** | The neighbour's actual name if they gave permission, then the route: "you're two streets from someone I already take care of." | Route compactness is the strongest yes in the business |
| **Tri-State Grill Cleaning warm list** 🔶 **CONDITIONAL** — access to that list is unconfirmed (`CANON.md` §10.5) | "You've had me on the property. This is the rest of what I do now." | The trust problem is already solved; skip straight to scope, season, and price |
| **Yard sign or door hanger** | The short form of *the sixteenth visit*, plus the open slot count | Recognition, not conversion (`brand-brief.md` §7) |
| **Google Business Profile** | What it is, where, the season, and the two published project prices | They are searching a category or a name they half-remember |
| **A project customer** (grill clean, pressure wash) | Nothing, on the day. Do the job. Mention the membership on the summary or the next contact. | Selling a membership during a paid project reads as a pitch and damages the project |

## 7. Three things never to say, at any point, on any surface

1. **Anything implying growth.** No "as we grow," no "currently accepting," no "additional
   territories," no "expanding to serve more families." `CANON.md` §1 — this is a deliberate side
   income and it is not becoming anything else. Copy that hints otherwise makes the six-client cap
   read as a startup phase instead of the product.
2. **Anything aimed at Westwood, Price Hill, or Cheviot.** No copy, no ads, no yard-sign plan, no
   service-area line that includes them. `CANON.md` §5 — this is arithmetic, not a judgment, and an
   inquiry from one of those areas that qualifies on attributes and route is welcome. An
   *exception* is fine; a *strategy* is not.
3. 🆕 **Anything about the pool that survives without the disclaimer.** The pool disclaimer travels
   in the same block as every description of pool work, on every surface, including alt text,
   captions, service lists, and summary templates. `voice.md` §8 item 41 is the audit test. This is
   the single highest-consequence rule added on 2026-08-05, because the scope change made the
   dangerous claims sound true.
